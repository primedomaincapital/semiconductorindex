What Moves Semiconductor Stocks: Earnings, Orders, or the AI Trade?

WHAT MOVES CHIPS · 10 MIN READ

On the morning of November 5, 2026, AMD had just reported the best quarter in its history: revenue of $13.4 billion, up 45 percent year over year, data-center revenue of $8.5 billion, up 97 percent, and guidance for the fourth quarter of about $17 billion. By the close that day, AMD's stock had fallen 17.26 percent, and the PHLX Semiconductor Index had suffered its worst session since March 2020, down 12.25 percent to 9,011.14. Nvidia lost about $450 billion in market value. Micron fell about 17 percent, SanDisk 22 percent, Arm 13 percent. The earnings were superb. The stocks collapsed anyway. If earnings drive chip stocks, somebody forgot to tell the market.

The disconnect is the central puzzle of semiconductor investing in 2026, and it has a sharper answer than most explanations allow. Chip stocks are moved by four forces — earnings, order books, memory prices, and the AI trade — but the four do not take turns. They overlap, they amplify each other, and in 2026 they pointed in different directions at the same time. Sorting out which one actually moved prices, and which merely moved with them, is the difference between understanding the sector and being carried by it.

Earnings: the anchor that slipped

Start with the force that is supposed to matter most. Through the first ten months of 2026, semiconductor earnings were extraordinary by any standard. TSMC reported a 69 percent jump in quarterly net profit and forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $52.3 to $54.3 billion. AMD's data-center business nearly doubled. Nvidia's quarterly revenue ran at a scale — $96 billion — that would have been unimaginable for a chip company five years earlier. Across the sector, the earnings behind one share of SMH, the VanEck Semiconductor ETF, rose 112 percent in a year, while the fund's price rose only 33 percent over the same stretch, according to ETF Copilot's analysis of data to November 9. SMH's price-to-earnings ratio fell from 40.6 times trailing earnings a year earlier to 25.5 times — below its median of 27.1 times since 2017. SOXX went the same way: its earnings rose 119 percent in the year and its price 43 percent, so its multiple fell from just over 40 times. By November, chip stocks were cheaper on earnings than they had been in years, and still falling.

That is the first clue. Earnings explain where chip stocks end up over years; they do a poor job of explaining where they go over weeks. The SOX more than doubled in the first half of 2026, reaching a record 14,634.72 on June 22, on earnings that were already strong. Then it gave back nearly 40 percent of that peak by November 5, on earnings that were stronger still. The multiple did the moving, not the earnings — expanding to about 50 times for SMH in June, then compressing back below its long-run median. When the multiple is the moving part, the question is not what the companies earned. It is what investors were willing to pay for those earnings, and what changed their minds.

The order book that isn't public

Chip companies do not publish order books the way an aircraft maker does, so the market reads them through a proxy: the capital spending of the four companies that buy most of the AI hardware. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta together guide to roughly $720 to $740 billion of capital expenditure for 2026 — about double what they spent in 2025, and more than triple their roughly $225 billion of 2024. In the July-to-September quarter alone, the four paid about $202 billion in cash for property and equipment, more than double the $97.3 billion of the same quarter in 2025. Amazon accounts for the largest share, about $220 billion for the year; Alphabet raised its range to $195 to $205 billion in October; Microsoft expects about $170 billion; Meta narrowed its range to $135 to $145 billion. Goldman Sachs estimates roughly $180 billion of that 2026 spending will go to GPUs and AI accelerators. McKinsey puts the total data-center investment on pace at $6.7 trillion by 2030, of which $5.2 trillion is AI-specific capacity.

That spending is the order book, and for most of 2026 it explained the rally better than any earnings release. Every upward revision to a hyperscaler's budget was a purchase order for Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, TSMC, and the memory makers — and the stocks moved on the guidance, not on the shipments. The Marvell agreement to buy Lumentum for an implied enterprise value of $115 billion, announced in the same week as the November selloff, was the largest public bet yet that AI connectivity is moving from copper to light — a deal priced on future orders, not current revenue. Reuters reported that advanced packaging remains a visible bottleneck and that Nvidia is said to be financing billions of dollars of capacity at SPIL. When the constraint on the industry is physical supply rather than demand, the order book is the story.

But the same proxy explains the selloff. The spending is financed with debt at a scale that has no precedent, and the returns on it are unproven. The neocloud model — borrow billions, buy GPUs, rent out compute — works only while utilization stays high. Gartner's analysis, cited by CNBC, argues AI revenue will be insufficient to support profitable returns on planned AI capital spending. In October, concerns about CoreWeave's financial condition, the AI cloud provider that has become a bellwether for AI infrastructure financing, helped trigger the first leg down. On November 4, midterm results raised fears that new policies could slow data-center construction; Meta's president said builds would be harder after the elections, and investors noted that Texas holds about 27 percent of US data-center gigawatts, watching whether a moratorium pledge becomes policy. The order book did not shrink on November 5. The market's confidence that the order book would be paid for did.

Memory prices: the factor that moved the index

If one force did the most measurable work in 2026, it was memory prices. Gartner's August revision of its forecast is the starkest single document of the year: global semiconductor revenue of $1,555.2 billion in 2026, up 92 percent from $809 billion in 2025, with memory revenue of $837.3 billion — 54 percent of the total, up from 27 percent a year earlier. DRAM revenue is forecast to rise 246.6 percent in 2026; NAND flash, 371.9 percent. Non-memory revenue, the rest of the industry — logic, analog, sensors, everything else — grows 21.9 percent, to $717.9 billion. In 2027, Gartner expects the total to reach $1,939.9 billion, with memory passing $1 trillion at $1,075.5 billion. The AI data-center ecosystem is expected to grow from 36.5 percent of semiconductor revenue in 2026 to more than 53 percent by 2030.

Read those numbers as a decomposition of the index's year and the picture sharpens. The 2026 chip rally was, to a remarkable extent, a memory-price rally: the memory makers' revenue nearly quadrupling while the rest of the industry grew at a solid but ordinary pace. That is why Micron fell about 17 percent and SanDisk 22 percent on November 5, more than the index itself — the stocks most levered to the price surge were most exposed when the financing story turned. It is also why the SOX's 2026 path — doubling to June, then falling 38.4 percent from the record by November 5 — tracks the memory cycle more closely than it tracks the AI earnings cycle. Memory prices are the most cyclical variable in the sector, and the index, for all its talk of AI, is still hostage to the oldest cycle in chips.

The distinction matters for what comes next. Memory price inflation — Gartner's term is memflation — is profound but not perennial, and the firm expects no meaningful pricing relief until late 2027. A market that rose on memory scarcity will eventually have to live with memory supply, and the three names at the center of the surge — Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron — will carry the index down when the cycle turns, just as they carried it up.

The counterargument: it really is just earnings, on a longer clock

There is a strong reply to all of this, and it deserves its weight. Over any horizon longer than a few months, earnings are the only force that has ever explained chip-stock returns, and 2026 is no exception to that rule — it is a demonstration of it. The earnings behind SMH rose 112 percent in a year while the price rose 33 percent. That is not a failure of earnings to matter. It is earnings mattering so much that the price had to fall to meet them: the market had priced in a multiple of 50 times, and the earnings arrived at a multiple of 25. The November selloff, on this reading, is not evidence that earnings stopped driving the market. It is evidence that valuations, not earnings, were the excess — and valuations corrected.

The capex story fits the same frame. Hyperscaler spending of $720 to $740 billion is not a separate force from earnings; it is the demand that produces the earnings, one or two quarters later. The order book and the earnings report are the same number seen at different points in time. And the AI trade, for all its talk of politics and credit, is ultimately a bet that the earnings will keep coming. If they keep compounding at triple-digit rates, the November drawdown will look like the October one: $1.64 trillion lost in six sessions, the SOX down 29 percent from its peak, and the index still up sharply on the year.

What actually moved prices, and what to watch

Put the four forces in order and the hierarchy is clear enough to act on. Over days and weeks, the AI trade and the credit behind it moved prices: the November 3 rebound of 2.63 percent, the November 4 fall of 3.13 percent on election results, the November 5 collapse of 12.25 percent, the November 6 recovery of 4.21 percent to 9,390.25 — none of those moves had an earnings release behind them. Over quarters, memory prices did the heavy lifting: a 246.6 percent rise in DRAM revenue and a 371.9 percent rise in NAND are the largest single-year moves in the sector's history, and they account for most of the index's 2026 gain. Over years, earnings remain the anchor — the 112 percent rise in SMH's earnings per share is the reason the index is still up roughly 25 to 39 percent on the year even after losing more than a third of its peak value.

That hierarchy suggests what to watch, and in what order. First, hyperscaler capital-spending guidance: the next cut or raise in the $720 to $740 billion budget will move the index before any earnings release does, and the November 17 Nvidia conference call — the first dated test after the selloff — was judged on guidance, not the quarter. Second, memory contract prices: if DRAM and NAND pricing holds into 2027, the memory makers carry the index; if it breaks, they drag it. Third, the financing chain: the neocloud borrowers, the data-center politics in Texas and beyond, and the credit conditions that decide whether the order book gets paid for. Earnings come last on the watch list, not because they matter least, but because they matter slowest — they are the destination the other three forces are arguing about. The 12 percent day did not prove earnings irrelevant. It proved that in the chip market, the price you pay for earnings can change faster than the earnings themselves.
