The 12 Percent Day: What November Said About the AI Trade

THE NOVEMBER SELLOFF  ·  5 MIN READ

Thursday, November 5, 2026, was the day the AI trade stopped being about earnings and started being about politics. The PHLX Semiconductor Index fell 12.25 percent in a single session — its worst day since March 2020 — closing at 9,011.14. Nvidia lost about $450 billion in market value. AMD fell 17.26 percent. Micron fell about 17 percent, SanDisk 22 percent, Arm 13 percent. The leveraged 3x fund SOXL fell 38.7 percent in a day. And the remarkable thing, reading the coverage afterward, is what nobody could point to: no cancelled chip order, no cut to a hyperscaler's spending plan, no earnings miss big enough to explain it. The index fell because of what might happen, not what had.

How a record became a bear market in five weeks

The numbers frame the speed of the reversal. The SOX closed at 13,217.8 on October 6 and at a record 14,634.72 on June 22. By the November 5 close it sat 38.4 percent below that June record — well past the 20 percent line that defines a bear market. Chip and memory stocks had lost about $3.7 trillion in market value since October 6, according to Yahoo Finance. Of 26 chip and memory stocks in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100, 22 closed at least 20 percent below their 2026 highs and 12 at least 50 percent below. The week itself was violent in both directions: the SOX rose 2.63 percent on November 3 as chip stocks rebounded ahead of AMD's results, fell 3.13 percent on November 4 after the midterm results raised fears that new policies could slow data-center construction, then collapsed on November 5 and recovered only part of the drop on November 6, closing at 9,390.25, up 4.21 percent on the day — still 12.2 percent below the prior Friday's close.

The proximate triggers were small. Qualcomm and Arm reported Wednesday night, and the results were read as mixed. Then the market turned to a different question: what Democratic control of both chambers of Congress, and a wave of local votes against data centers, means for the build-out. Meta's president said data-center builds would be harder after the elections. Wells Fargo estimates Texas holds about 27 percent of US data-center gigawatts, and investors watched whether the Texas moratorium pledge becomes policy. The selling concentrated exactly where the AI story lives: Nvidia closed at $181.60, down 9.26 percent, its biggest one-day drop since April 2025. The Nasdaq entered a correction the same day. On Friday, November 6, the bill arrived in Taipei: the Taiex fell as much as 9 percent intraday before the committee running Taiwan's National Financial Stabilization Fund authorized its NT$500 billion pool — about $15 billion — to intervene. TSMC is more than 40 percent of the Taiwan market's capitalization.

The fundamentals refused to cooperate with the panic

Here is what makes November 5 different from an ordinary correction: the companies kept reporting superb numbers while their stocks fell. AMD reported third-quarter revenue of $13.4 billion, up 45 percent year over year, with record data-center revenue of $8.5 billion, up 97 percent, and guided fourth-quarter revenue to about $17 billion. Marvell agreed to buy Lumentum for an implied enterprise value of $115 billion — 4.5 Marvell shares for each Lumentum share — the largest public bet yet that AI connectivity is moving from copper to light. Phison, after its own record quarter, locked priority access to DRAM through the end of 2029. Reuters reported that advanced packaging remains a visible bottleneck and that Nvidia is said to be financing billions of dollars of capacity at SPIL. Memory is physically scarce. Demand is not the problem. Financing is — or, more precisely, the fear that the financing of the AI build-out, including the debt of neocloud companies that rent out AI computing capacity, cannot support the spending plans already announced. Gartner's analysis, cited by CNBC, argues AI revenue will be insufficient to support profitable returns on planned AI capital spending.

The counterargument: the market may be right to worry

It is tempting to call November 5 an overreaction, and in the narrow sense it was — no order was cancelled that day. But the worry has a real foundation. The AI build-out is being financed with debt at a scale that has no precedent, and the returns on that spending are unproven. The neocloud model — borrow billions, buy GPUs, rent out compute — works only while utilization stays high and chip prices stay high. If data-center construction slows for political reasons, utilization falls, and the leveraged buyers are the first to sell. SOXL's 86.4 percent collapse from its June close is the leveraged version of the same fear. The index is not pricing this quarter's earnings. It is pricing the probability that the financing chain breaks before the revenue arrives.

That is also why the index has become so hard to read. The SOX now trades three things at once: chip demand, data-center politics, and credit conditions in the AI supply chain. A single index cannot tell you which of the three moved on any given day, and November 5 moved all three in the same direction. The October selloff that this site covered last month — $1.64 trillion lost in six sessions, the SOX down 29 percent from its peak — was the first warning. November was the second, louder one.

What this means for anyone watching the index

The next dated test is November 17, when Nvidia hosts its fiscal 2027 third-quarter conference call. The question will not be the quarter — it will be guidance, and whether any hyperscaler trims its capital spending plan in the weeks after. Until then, the honest read is that the semiconductor index has stopped being a pure technology benchmark and become a political and credit instrument as well. That is precisely the problem an index is supposed to solve: when the benchmark itself becomes the story, the market needs better ways to measure what it owns. The 12 percent day did not settle whether the AI trade is over. It settled that the trade is no longer only about chips.
