Chip Stocks vs. the Index: What Active Semiconductor Managers Actually Beat

ACTIVE VS. INDEX · 9 MIN READ

For the six months ending August 31, 2026, Fidelity's Select Semiconductors Portfolio gained 36.48 percent. Its benchmark, the MSCI US IMI Semiconductors and Semiconductor Equipment 25/50 Index, gained 37.06 percent. The fund trailed by 0.58 points — and that narrow miss contains the whole argument about active management in chips. Two of the fund's holdings, Marvell Technology and Astera Labs, rose 152 and 151 percent respectively over the same six months. The managers picked two of the year's great winners, and still finished behind the index. In semiconductors, picking winners is not the hard part. Picking enough of them, in the right sizes, is.

The question behind that result — do semiconductor funds beat the PHLX index? — has a different answer depending on the window you choose, and the windows disagree with each other in ways that should make any investor suspicious of a single number. Over a decade, the active managers look like geniuses. Over 2026, they look like they missed the trade entirely. Both readings are true, and neither is the whole story.

The 2026 scoreboard is brutal for the stock-pickers

Start with the year that is still running. As of November 19, 2026, the Fidelity fund — ticker FSELX, $44.84 billion in assets at the end of August, run by Sonu Kalra since July 15 with Aidan Brandt joining as co-manager on August 27 — had returned about 22.5 percent for the year. The iShares Semiconductor ETF, SOXX, had returned about 41.5 percent over the same stretch, according to PortfoliosLab's comparison of the two funds. That is a gap of roughly 19 points, and it is not a rounding error or a fee artifact. FSELX charges 0.60 percent a year, against about a third of a percent for the index funds; fees explain a fraction of a point, not nineteen.

The index funds themselves were not standing still. Finnhub's market data as of November 19 put SOXX's year-to-date price return at 38.65 percent, the Invesco PHLX Semiconductor ETF (SOXQ) at 34.21 percent, and the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH) at 25.49 percent. Over one year, SOXX was up 55.74 percent and SMH 39.01 percent. On total returns with dividends reinvested, from December 31, 2025 to October 30, 2026, SOXX rose 56.8 percent, SOXQ 51.2 percent, and SMH 45.1 percent. The dispersion among the index funds — nearly twelve points between the best and worst of the three — is itself a reminder that "the index" is three different rulebooks, not one. But every one of them beat the flagship active fund in 2026, some by a wide margin.

The drawdown tells the same story from the other side. In 2026 so far, the deepest drop for FSELX was 38.12 percent, against 39.55 percent for SOXX. Active management did not protect the downside either. When the SOX fell 12.25 percent on November 5 — its worst day since March 2020 — and SMH lost 11 percent in the session, the active funds fell with the market. From October 6, the day the Nasdaq set its record, to November 9, SMH lost 23 percent and SOXX 25 percent. A manager who could not dodge that slide had to beat the index on the way up to earn the fee. In 2026, the Fidelity fund did not.

The ten-year scoreboard says the opposite

Lengthen the window and the picture inverts. Over the past ten years, FSELX returned about 29.8 percent a year, against about 28.1 percent a year for SOXX, on PortfoliosLab's figures — a gap of roughly 1.7 points a year, compounded over a decade. The yearly record shows where it came from. In 2021, FSELX gained 59.16 percent against SOXX's 44.09 percent. In 2023, it gained 78.49 percent against 67.12 percent. In 2024, the gap was enormous: FSELX up 43.61 percent while SOXX rose just 12.92 percent. In 2025, FSELX gained 43.32 percent against 40.74 percent. The fund lost only one year outright in that run — 2022, when both fell about 35 percent — and even then the difference was 0.18 points.

The trailing twelve months to August 31, 2026 tell the same story in miniature: FSELX was up 83.42 percent, slightly ahead of its MSCI benchmark, and well ahead of the broad market and its peer-group average. Fidelity's own commentary credits the outperformance to holdings in the semiconductor subindustry — the same area where, over the six-month window, its security selection detracted. The contradiction is only apparent. Over twelve months the fund's bets paid; over six, they did not quite. Active returns in this sector arrive in lumps, and the lumps are large.

There is a second active-versus-index comparison hiding inside the index funds themselves. SMH and SOXX are both passive, but SMH's concentrated 25-name rulebook — Nvidia at about 20 percent — is itself a bet on the biggest names. Over the ten years from November 2016 to November 2026, SMH returned 1,519.1 percent against SOXX's 1,188 percent. Since July 2001, SMH has compounded at about 13.3 percent a year against SOXX's 12.8 percent. The "index" that won the decade was the one that behaved most like an active manager with a single conviction: own more Nvidia.

Where the active wins actually came from

That observation points to the mechanism. A semiconductor index is, by construction, a committee: 25 or 30 names, caps on the largest, a rebuild once or twice a year. An active manager is not bound by any of that. The manager can own more Nvidia than the SOX's 12 percent cap allows, can hold a company the index has not yet admitted, can sell a name the index is forced to keep until September. In a sector where the dispersion between winners and losers is extreme — Marvell up 152 percent in six months while other chip names fell by half from their highs — the freedom to size positions is worth more than the freedom to choose names. The index owns all thirty; the manager owns the ten that matter and sizes them like it.

The 2024 result is the cleanest demonstration. SOXX rose 12.92 percent in a year when FSELX rose 43.61 percent — a gap of more than thirty points. That is not a fee story or a luck story. It is a concentration story: the active fund held larger positions in the year's winners than any capped index could, and the capped index diluted those winners with the rest of the sector. The same mechanism explains 2021 and 2023. When a handful of chip stocks drive the entire sector's return, the manager who can overweight them wins. When the whole sector rises together, as it did for much of 2026, the index — which owns everything, including the names the manager skipped — wins instead.

There is also the matter of what the active fund is measured against. FSELX benchmarks itself to an MSCI index, not the PHLX, and its managers note that the fund's holdings represented about 80 percent of that industry index. The comparison with SOXX is therefore a comparison across benchmarks as much as across management styles. A fund can beat its own benchmark and still trail a different index fund, and that is exactly what happened in the six months to August 31: FSELX trailed its MSCI benchmark by 0.58 points while trailing SOXX by far more. The scoreboard depends on which index is doing the scoring.

The counterargument: the index has the better deal

The case for the index is not that active managers lack skill. It is that the skill is expensive, inconsistent, and — in the worst years — absent exactly when it is needed most. FSELX charges 0.60 percent a year, roughly double the index funds' 0.33 to 0.35 percent, and the manager changed in mid-2026: Kalra took over on July 15, Brandt joined on August 27. A ten-year record built by earlier managers is not a promise about the next ten. The 2026 gap of nineteen points is the kind of underperformance that takes years of outperformance to repair, and it arrived in a year when the index funds themselves were up 25 to 42 percent. Missing a rally that large is worse than missing a flat year; the opportunity cost compounds.

And the downside protection that active management is supposed to provide did not show up. FSELX's maximum drawdown in 2026, 38.12 percent, was barely better than SOXX's 39.55 percent. In 2022, both fell about 35 percent. The active fund charges for judgment and delivers, in the drawdowns, roughly the index's result. The honest summary of the record is not that active managers cannot beat the PHLX. It is that they beat it in the years when concentration pays — 2021, 2023, 2024 — and lose to it in the years when breadth pays, like 2026, and that no investor can know in advance which kind of year is coming.

When stock-picking has added value

The pattern that emerges is specific enough to use. Active semiconductor management has added value when returns were concentrated in a few names the index caps — the AI accelerator build-out, the memory cycle turns, the equipment makers riding a fab-construction wave — and when the manager was willing to hold those names at weights no index would permit. It has subtracted value when the whole sector moved together, when the winners were the index's largest names at their capped weights, and when the manager's own caution kept the fund out of the rally. The 2024 gap of thirty points and the 2026 gap of nineteen points in the other direction are the same phenomenon seen from opposite sides: in chips, the distance between the best and worst outcome is not a few points. It is a third of the portfolio.

That is the real lesson of the scoreboard. The semiconductor index is not a neutral measuring stick that active managers either clear or fail. It is a concentrated, capped, periodically rebuilt basket of the most volatile sector in the market — and beating it requires a bet at least as concentrated as the index itself. The managers who made that bet in 2021, 2023, and 2024 earned their fees several times over. The managers who did not make it in 2026 paid for the omission. Anyone choosing between the two in 2027 is not choosing between skill and its absence. They are choosing which kind of concentration they would rather own — the index's, set by a rulebook, or a manager's, set by conviction — and whether they believe the next year will reward the few or the many.
